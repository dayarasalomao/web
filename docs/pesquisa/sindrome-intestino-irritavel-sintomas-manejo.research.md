## 1. Definition

- IBS is a chronic disorder of gut–brain interaction, previously called a functional GI disorder. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- Defined by recurrent abdominal pain with disordered defecation, not by any structural finding. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- **Rome IV criteria, verbatim:** "Recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 d/wk in the last 3 mo, associated with 2 or more of the following criteria: 1. Related to defecation. 2. Associated with a change in the frequency of stool. 3. Associated with a change in the form (appearance) of stool." Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months, symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, Table 1; adapted there from Rome IV, Gastroenterology 2016;150:1393–407)
- Bloating is commonly reported but **is not required** for the diagnosis. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- BSG patient-facing framing: "problems arising between the gut and the brain… Problems in IBS have been found in the nervous system supplying the gut, often making it more sensitive." Psychological factors, certain foods and gut microbes "can all play a role in triggering symptoms". Can start after a gut infection or antibiotic use, but often there is no clear origin. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2, patient summary)

## 2. Who it affects

- Prevalence approximately **4.4%–4.8%** in the United States, United Kingdom and Canada. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- Most common in **women** and in people **younger than 50**. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- Direct US medical costs excluding prescription and OTC medication estimated at **$1.5–$10 billion per year**. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- Quality-of-life impact, as reported in Source #1: one study found a majority of patients would give up 10–15 years of life expectancy for an instant cure; another found patients would accept a median risk of sudden death of 1% from a hypothetical medication that could cure their symptoms. `[COHORT]` (Source #1, citing its refs 8–9) ⚠ VERIFICAR COM A MÉDICA — striking figures, cite carefully or omit; they are secondary citations, not read at source.
- **No Brazil-specific prevalence figure was retrieved in this run.** Do not state one. `[UNVERIFIED]`

## 3. Symptoms and natural history

- Core: recurrent abdominal pain plus change in stool frequency and/or form; relief or worsening of pain related to defecation. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- Subtypes are assigned by predominant stool consistency using the Bristol Stool Form Scale: IBS-C, IBS-D, IBS-M. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- The precise Rome IV subtype thresholds (>25% of bowel movements of a given Bristol form) appear in the ACG guideline text but were **not** on the pages read in this run. `[UNVERIFIED]` — verify against Source #1 before the page states percentages.
- Bloating is common but not diagnostic. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)
- BSG recommends the natural history and common symptom triggers be **explained to the patient** as part of management, framing IBS as a disorder of gut–brain interaction and covering diet, stress, cognitive/behavioural and emotional responses, and post-infectious changes. Recommendation: strong; quality of evidence: weak. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)

## 4. Red flags

Alarm features named by BSG's patient summary as prompting specialist referral: `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)

- bleeding from the back passage (rectal bleeding)
- substantial unintentional weight loss
- anaemia
- a strong family history of cancer
- older age at onset

Related, from the diagnostic-testing recommendations rather than a red-flag list: `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1)

- ACG frames its colonoscopy recommendation around patients "younger than 45 years **without warning signs**", which implies age ≥45 at onset and the presence of warning signs as the triggers for endoscopy.
- Fecal calprotectin/CRP testing is recommended specifically in patients **without alarm features** who have suspected IBS with diarrhoea — i.e. presence of alarm features moves the patient out of that pathway.

## 5. Diagnosis

**Positive diagnosis, not exclusion — the central point of both guidelines.**

- "We suggest a positive diagnostic strategy as compared to a diagnostic strategy of exclusion for patients with symptoms of IBS to improve time to initiate appropriate therapy." Consensus recommendation. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, rec 5)
- "We recommend a positive diagnostic strategy as compared to a diagnostic strategy of exclusion for patients with symptoms of IBS to improve cost-effectiveness." Strong recommendation; **high** quality of evidence. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, rec 6)

**Tests recommended:**

- Serologic testing to rule out **coeliac disease** in patients with IBS and diarrhoea. Strong; moderate. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, rec 1)
- **Fecal calprotectin** (or fecal lactoferrin) **and CRP** in patients without alarm features with suspected IBS-D, to rule out IBD. Strong; moderate for CRP and calprotectin, **very low** for lactoferrin. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, rec 2)
- BSG: FBC, CRP, coeliac serology and faecal calprotectin form the standard work-up; the patient summary describes "blood tests, including one to rule out coeliac disease… and, if diarrhoea is present, a stool test to rule out inflammation." `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)
- Anorectal physiology testing where IBS coexists with a defaecatory disorder or faecal incontinence, to select those who might benefit from biofeedback. Weak; low. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2). ACG parallels this for suspected pelvic floor dysfunction or refractory constipation not responsive to standard therapy. Consensus. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, rec 9)

**Tests the guidelines recommend AGAINST — the brief asks for these explicitly:**

- **Against routine colonoscopy** in patients with IBS symptoms **younger than 45 years without warning signs**. Conditional; low. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, rec 4)
- **Against routine stool testing for enteric pathogens** in all patients with IBS. Conditional; low. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, rec 3)
- **Against testing for food allergies and food sensitivities** in all patients with IBS, unless there are reproducible symptoms concerning for a food allergy. Consensus. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #1, rec 8)
- **No role** for exocrine pancreatic insufficiency testing, or for hydrogen breath testing to rule out small intestinal bacterial overgrowth or carbohydrate intolerance, in patients with typical IBS symptoms. Strong; weak. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)

## 6. Conservative vs procedural management

**IBS has no procedural/surgical management.** This is a medical and dietary condition; see section 10.

**First-line (BSG unless noted):** `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)

- Regular exercise advised for all patients. Strong; weak.
- First-line dietary advice offered to all patients. Strong; weak.
- **Soluble fibre** (e.g. ispaghula) effective for global symptoms and abdominal pain; **insoluble fibre (wheat bran) should be avoided** as it may exacerbate symptoms. Start low (3–4 g/day) and build gradually to avoid bloating. Strong; moderate. ACG agrees: soluble, not insoluble, fibre. Strong; moderate. (Source #1, rec 11)
- **Low FODMAP** diet as **second-line dietary** therapy, implementation supervised by a trained dietitian, with reintroduction according to tolerance. Weak; very low. ACG: limited trial of a low FODMAP diet. Conditional; very low. (Source #1, rec 10)
- **Gluten-free diet NOT recommended.** Weak; very low.
- **Food elimination diets based on IgG antibodies NOT recommended.** Strong; **moderate**.
- Loperamide for diarrhoea; abdominal pain, bloating, nausea and constipation are common and may limit tolerability. Strong; very low.
- Peppermint oil may be effective for global symptoms and abdominal pain; gastro-oesophageal reflux is a common side effect. Weak; very low. ACG: suggests peppermint. Conditional; low. (Source #1, rec 13)

**Second-line:** `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)

- **Tricyclic antidepressants** as gut–brain neuromodulators, for global symptoms and abdominal pain. Can be started in primary or secondary care. Start low (e.g. amitriptyline 10 mg once daily), titrate slowly to a maximum of 30–50 mg once daily. Careful explanation of the rationale is required and patients counselled on side effects. Strong; moderate. ACG concurs: TCAs to treat global IBS symptoms. Strong; moderate. (Source #1, rec 23)
- SSRIs as gut–brain neuromodulators, same counselling caveat. Weak; low.
- Rifaximin for IBS-D in secondary care; effect on abdominal pain is limited; licensed for this indication in the USA but not available for it in many countries. Weak; moderate. ACG: rifaximin for global IBS-D symptoms. Strong; moderate. (Source #1, rec 20)
- Secretagogues for IBS-C (linaclotide, lubiprostone, plecanatide, tenapanor) — several are "not yet available for this indication in many countries". Strong; high/moderate depending on agent.
- 5-HT3 antagonists for IBS-D (alosetron, ramosetron unavailable in many countries; ondansetron a reasonable alternative). Weak; moderate to high.
- Eluxadoline for IBS-D; contraindicated after cholecystectomy, in sphincter of Oddi problems, alcohol dependence, pancreatitis or severe liver impairment. Weak; moderate.

**Psychological therapies:** `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)

- IBS-specific CBT. Strong; low.
- Gut-directed hypnotherapy. Strong; low.
- Should be considered when symptoms have not improved after **12 months** of drug treatment; referral can be made earlier if accessible and based on patient preference. Strong; low.
- ACG: gut-directed psychotherapies for global IBS symptoms. Conditional; very low. (Source #1, rec 24)

**Severe or refractory IBS:** `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)

- Should prompt a review of the diagnosis. Weak; very low.
- Managed with an integrated multidisciplinary approach. Weak; very low.
- **"Iatrogenic harms due to opioid prescribing, unnecessary surgery and unproven unregulated diagnostic or therapeutic approaches incentivised by financial or reputational gain should be avoided."** Strong; very low.

## 9. Patient questions (FAQ) — answer sketches, not copy

1. **É "só" nervoso / é da minha cabeça?** — No. IBS is a disorder of gut–brain interaction with demonstrated changes in gut nervous system sensitivity; psychological factors are one contributor among several, not the cause. Source #2 (patient summary), Source #1.
2. **Preciso fazer colonoscopia para descobrir se é intestino irritável?** — Not routinely, under 45 and without warning signs. Source #1, rec 4.
3. **Que exames eu preciso fazer, então?** — Blood tests including coeliac serology, and if diarrhoea is present, faecal calprotectin/CRP to exclude inflammation. Sources #1 (recs 1–2), #2.
4. **O diagnóstico é só por exclusão?** — No: it is a positive diagnosis using Rome IV, and treating it as exclusion delays treatment and costs more. Source #1, recs 5–6.
5. **Teste de intolerância alimentar / IgG ajuda?** — Not recommended: IgG-based elimination diets are explicitly not recommended (strong, moderate), and allergy testing only where a genuine food allergy is suspected. Sources #2, #1 rec 8.
6. **Devo cortar glúten?** — A gluten-free diet is not recommended for IBS as such; coeliac disease is a different diagnosis that must be excluded first. Source #2; Source #1 rec 1.
7. **Fibra ajuda ou piora?** — Depends which: soluble fibre helps, insoluble (wheat bran) should be avoided; start low and build up. Sources #2, #1 rec 11.
8. **FODMAP funciona? Posso fazer sozinha?** — Second-line, and implementation should be supervised by a trained dietitian with structured reintroduction. Source #2.
9. **Probiótico resolve?** — Guidelines disagree; see section "conflict" below. Sources #1 rec 14, #2.
10. **Antidepressivo? Eu não estou deprimida.** — TCAs are used at low dose as gut–brain neuromodulators for pain, not as antidepressant therapy; the rationale needs explaining and the dose is far lower. Sources #2, #1 rec 23.
11. **Isso tem cura?** — Framing question. No cure claim is supportable from these sources; management targets symptoms and quality of life. ⚠ VERIFICAR COM A MÉDICA.
12. **Quando devo me preocupar?** — Rectal bleeding, substantial weight loss, anaemia, strong family history of cancer, older age at onset. Source #2.

## Conflicts between guidelines — report both, per the brief

| Topic | ACG (Source #1) | BSG (Source #2) |
|---|---|---|
| **Probiotics** | "We suggest **against** probiotics for the treatment of global IBS symptoms." Conditional; very low. (rec 14) | "Probiotics, as a group, **may be an effective** treatment… reasonable to advise patients wishing to try probiotics to take them for up to 12 weeks, and to discontinue if there is no improvement." Weak; very low. |
| **Antispasmodics** | "We recommend **against** the use of antispasmodics for the treatment of global IBS symptoms." Conditional; low. (rec 12) | "Certain antispasmodics **may be an effective** treatment for global symptoms and abdominal pain." Weak; very low. |
| **PEG (polyethylene glycol)** | "We suggest **against** PEG products to relieve global IBS symptoms in those with IBS-C." Conditional; low. (rec 15) | "Polyethylene glycol **may be an effective** treatment for constipation in IBS." Weak; very low. |

Note the scoping difference in the PEG and antispasmodic rows: ACG judges effect on **global IBS symptoms**, BSG on the **individual symptom** (constipation, pain). That is likely most of the disagreement, and the page should say so rather than pick a winner. Both bodies rate the evidence weak/low in every one of these rows.

## 10. Scope boundary

- IBS is managed primarily in **gastroenterology and primary care**. BSG explicitly locates first-line care in primary care, with several second-line drugs designated "in secondary care". `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)
- The coloproctology contribution is legitimate and specific: differentiating IBS from anorectal disease, evaluating a change in bowel habit, and **anorectal physiology testing and biofeedback where a defaecatory disorder or faecal incontinence coexists** — which is squarely coloproctology. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)
- **Colonoscopy is not performed by Dra. Dayara at this location.** Where a case does warrant endoscopy (alarm features, age ≥45 at onset), the page must describe referral, not imply the exam happens here. Per the brief's practice context.
- Dietary implementation (low FODMAP) is explicitly a **dietitian's** role. Do not imply the practice delivers it. `[GUIDELINE]` (Source #2)
- Several second-line drugs named by both guidelines are **not licensed or not available in many countries**. Whether any given agent is available in Brazil was **not** checked in this run — do not name specific drugs on the page without confirming Brazilian availability. `[UNVERIFIED]`

## 13. Do not state

- ❌ Any **cure** claim for IBS. Neither guideline supports one.
- ❌ Any **percentage of patients who improve** — no outcome rate was retrieved (section 8).
- ❌ Any **Brazilian prevalence figure** — none retrieved (section 2).
- ❌ That IBS is **diagnosed by exclusion**. Both guidelines say the opposite, and ACG grades the cost-effectiveness of the positive strategy as high-quality evidence.
- ❌ That **everyone with IBS needs a colonoscopy**, or that colonoscopy diagnoses IBS.
- ❌ That **IgG food-intolerance panels** guide treatment. Explicitly not recommended, strong recommendation on moderate evidence — one of the firmest negatives in either document.
- ❌ That a **gluten-free diet** treats IBS.
- ❌ That **probiotics work**, or that they do not — guidelines conflict; present both.
- ❌ That **insoluble fibre / wheat bran** is good for IBS. BSG says it should be avoided.
- ❌ Naming **specific second-line drugs as available** without checking Brazilian licensing.
- ❌ That the practice performs **colonoscopy** or delivers **dietitian-led FODMAP** implementation.
- ❌ Any framing that IBS is **psychological** or "nervoso" in the dismissive sense.
- ❌ CFM: no superiority claim over other doctors or services, no guaranteed outcome, no sensationalist framing of symptoms.
